Research programme

Building the substrate, and the workloads that test it.

Our two divisions ask the same question from opposite ends: what does it take to run a computation that did not exist a few years ago. The computer science division builds execution platforms for deep learning and quantum hardware. The data science division builds and studies the language and multimodal models that keep moving the target.

Division 01

Computer science — execution platforms

A model or a circuit is a description of intent. Everything between that description and a finished result is our subject: what is decided ahead of time, what is decided while running, and what it costs to be wrong.

01

Compiler stacks for deep learning

Graph capture, operator fusion, kernel selection and memory planning. We are interested in decisions that survive a change of model family — and in the cases where the compiler should hand control back to the runtime rather than guess.

Typical questions: when does fusion stop paying? What does a scheduler need to know about a shape it will only see at run time?

IR designFusionAutotuningMemory planning
02

Distributed training and serving

Parallelism strategy, collective communication, checkpointing and elasticity on clusters that are shared, heterogeneous and occasionally unreliable. We measure end to end, including the failures, because the tail is where the cost lives.

Typical questions: how should a serving system trade latency against utilisation when the request mix shifts hourly?

ParallelismCollectivesElasticityInference serving
03

Quantum compilation and control

Circuit synthesis, qubit mapping and routing under real device topologies, noise-aware scheduling, and the software layer that turns a scheduled circuit into pulses. The constraints are harsher than in classical compilation, which makes the trade-offs easier to see.

Typical questions: what does a compiler do differently when every additional gate costs fidelity?

Mapping & routingNoise-awarePulse controlError mitigation
04

Hybrid classical–quantum orchestration

Variational and iterative workloads put a QPU inside a classical loop. We work on scheduling, queueing and cost models that treat both resources as one system rather than a fast machine waiting on a slow one.

Typical questions: where should the loop live, and what is the right unit of work to send across the boundary?

SchedulersCost modelsRuntime systems
Division 02

Data science — language and multimodal models

We train models, take them apart, and try to say precisely what changed and why. The work is empirical by temperament: a claim that cannot be measured on something other than the benchmark it was tuned on is not yet a result.

01

Training and steering language models

Data curation, pre-training and post-training recipes, and the reasoning behaviour they produce. We are especially interested in separating genuine generalisation from the well-dressed memorisation that looks just like it on a leaderboard.

Typical questions: which post-training choices move capability, and which only move the score?

Pre-training dataPost-trainingReasoningAlignment
02

Multimodal representation

Speech, vision and text in a single model: how modalities are aligned, how much transfers across languages, and what breaks first in the low-resource setting. Multilingual speech is a recurring test bed because it exposes shortcuts quickly.

Typical questions: is the model reasoning across modalities, or has one modality quietly become decorative?

SpeechVision-languageCross-modal alignmentLow-resource
03

Efficiency as a research variable

Distillation, quantisation, sparsity and caching — studied with the serving system in view rather than as isolated tricks. This is where the two divisions meet most often, and where a systems result and a modelling result are usually the same result.

Typical questions: what does a quality/latency curve look like when the deployment is honest about batch size?

DistillationQuantisationInference cost
04

Evaluation and failure analysis

Benchmarks that keep meaning something after release: contamination, construct validity, and evaluations designed so that a failure tells you which assumption was wrong. We publish the cases we could not fix along with the ones we could.

Typical questions: what does this number predict about behaviour outside the test set — and how would we know?

Benchmark designContaminationError analysis
Practice

How the work gets done

Measured, not asserted

Every systems claim comes with an end-to-end number on hardware we describe. Microbenchmarks are evidence, not conclusions.

Artifacts by default

Code, configurations and traces are released with the paper. If a result cannot be reproduced from the repository, it is not finished.

Two divisions, one review

Systems drafts get read by model people and the reverse. It is slower, and it catches the assumptions each side forgot it was making.

Looking for a problem to work on?

Each thread above has open questions we would happily hand to a new student or a visiting collaborator.
